How to Find Affiliate Products to Promote: A Beginner's 10-Question Checklist

Finding affiliate products to promote is easy. Every marketplace has thousands. The hard part, especially for beginners, is deciding which one deserves your name on it. Here are the ten yes/no questions to ask before you promote anything.

Why the offer matters more than the hook

Most beginners spend their energy on content: the perfect hook, the right hashtags, the best time to post. But the product you recommend shapes everything that comes after it. A good fit makes your content easier to write and easier to stand behind. A poor fit wastes weeks, can get refunded (which can reverse your commission), and costs you the trust of the few people who were paying attention.

So before you write a single caption, run the offer through a short checklist. The goal is not to find a "winning" product. No checklist can promise that. The goal is to catch the obvious problems early, so you say no to the wrong offers quickly.

Where to find affiliate products and programs to promote

You find candidates in three common places:

  • Affiliate marketplaces such as Digistore24 or ClickBank, which list many digital products in one place. Some listings show performance numbers. Digistore24, for example, may show figures like cart conversion, net earnings per sale and cancellation rate, and ClickBank shows a "gravity" score.
  • Retailer programs such as Amazon Associates, where you can link to physical products and commission rates depend on the product category.
  • Direct programs run by a single company, usually linked from the footer of its website as "Affiliates" or "Partners".

Wherever you look, the same rule applies: shortlist two or three candidates, then test each one against the questions below. Don't commit to the first thing that looks exciting.

How to choose an affiliate product: 10 questions to ask first

Answer each one yes or no for a single offer. Three of them are marked must be yes. If any of those three is a no, skip the offer, however good the numbers look.

The product and its page

1. Can you see a clear price, what the buyer gets, and how it is delivered?

A buyer should know what they are paying and what arrives afterwards before they reach checkout. If the price or the contents are hidden until the last step, treat that as a warning sign.

2. Does the page show a real company or person, with a way to contact them?

Look for a business name, a support email or contact page, and a refund or terms page. If something goes wrong for your buyer, someone has to be reachable.

3. Is the page free of guaranteed-income claims, fake-looking testimonials and countdown timers that reset? must be yes

Refresh the page and watch the timer. Read the testimonials and ask whether they could be checked. "Guaranteed results" and "make $X a day" promises are a hard no. When you promote an offer, its claims become claims you are repeating.

4. Could you repeat every claim on the page truthfully, in your own words?

Read the sales page as a skeptical buyer would. If you wouldn't be comfortable saying a claim out loud to a friend, you shouldn't be sending people to it.

The money

5. Do you know exactly what you earn per sale, after any fees?

Write the number down. A "50% commission" on a low-priced product can be a small amount once fees are taken out. Knowing the real figure helps you decide whether the offer is worth your time. Our free affiliate commission calculator does the arithmetic, including refunds.

6. Is there a clear refund policy, and a refund or cancellation rate you are comfortable with?

A refund policy protects buyers, which is good. But refunded sales can reduce or reverse your commission, and a high refund rate can mean the product doesn't deliver what the page promises. Many beginners use around 10% as a point to start being cautious, but treat that as a rule of thumb, not a law.

7. Is there any evidence the offer sells?

Marketplace statistics, a sales history, or a direct answer from the vendor all count. These numbers describe what happened for other affiliates in the past. They don't predict what will happen for you, and new products may have almost no data. When the data is thin, lean harder on questions 1 to 4.

You and your audience

8. Have you read the affiliate terms, including what you may not say or do? must be yes

Programs often restrict things like paid ads on the brand name, certain claims, or where you can post links. Breaking the terms can mean losing commissions you already earned.

9. Are you willing to say clearly, every time, that you may earn a commission? must be yes

In the US, the FTC expects affiliate relationships to be disclosed clearly, and platforms like TikTok and Instagram have their own disclosure rules. See our affiliate disclosure examples for wording you can copy. If you would feel awkward disclosing a particular product, that tells you something about it.

10. Can you say in one sentence who this offer is for, and who it is not for?

"This is for busy parents who want a simple weekly meal plan. It's not for anyone on a medical diet." If you can't write that sentence, you don't know the product well enough to recommend it yet.

Common mistakes when choosing your first affiliate product

  • Picking by commission size alone. A big commission on a product that gets refunded pays nothing.
  • Choosing a niche you can't talk about for months. Pick a topic you can learn about and speak about honestly over time.
  • Starting in high-risk niches. Health, weight loss and "make money online" offers attract exaggerated claims and regulator attention. Anything that needs professional qualifications to advise on, such as medical, legal or investment products, is best avoided while you're starting out.
  • Ignoring how people buy. Cheap, simple products are often impulse purchases, and a short clear post can be enough. Expensive or complex ones are research purchases, where people want comparisons and detail before they buy. Match your content to the kind of purchase.

Questions beginners ask

How do I choose an affiliate program?

Use the same ten questions on the program as a whole: read its terms, check what you earn per sale after fees, look at its refund policy, and make sure a real company with a contact address runs it. Then check the individual product you would promote, because a good program can still include weak products.

How many products should I promote at first?

One. Shortlist two or three, run each through the checklist, and start with the one that passes most cleanly. Adding a second offer makes more sense once you understand how your first one performs.

Do I need a website to promote an affiliate offer?

No. Many beginners share offers through short-form video and a link in their profile bio. You still need to follow the program's terms and the platform's rules, and disclose the affiliate relationship every time.

Is a high-converting offer always a good choice?

No. Conversion figures are past results from other affiliates, not a prediction. An offer can convert well and still fail question 3 or have a high refund rate.

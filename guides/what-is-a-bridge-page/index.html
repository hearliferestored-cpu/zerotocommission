What Is a Bridge Page? A Beginner's Guide With Examples

A bridge page is one of the first terms beginners run into in affiliate marketing, and one of the most misused. Here's what it is, how it differs from a landing page and a sales page, four filled-in examples, and when you don't need one at all.

What is a bridge page?

A bridge page is a short page an affiliate puts between their content and the product's sales page. Someone taps your link, lands on the bridge page, reads a short, honest explanation of the offer and your disclosure, then clicks through to the seller's page if they're still interested.

Some people call it a presell page. The idea is the same: it "bridges" the gap between what someone saw in your video and a sales page they've never seen before.

Bridge page vs landing page vs sales page

PageWho makes itIts job
Bridge pageYou, the affiliateExplain the offer in your own words, say who it suits and who it doesn't, disclose, and link to the sales page.
Landing pageAnyoneA general term for any page built for one action, such as signing up or clicking through. A bridge page is one kind of landing page.
Squeeze (opt-in) pageUsually youAsk for an email address in exchange for something free. Some affiliates put an opt-in on their bridge page; then email rules apply.
Sales pageThe seller (vendor)Sell the product and lead to checkout. As an affiliate you don't control what it says.

How a bridge page fits with short-form video

For a faceless short-form video account, the path usually looks like this:

  1. Your video teaches or explains something and mentions where to find more.
  2. Your profile link (the one clickable link most short-form platforms give you).
  3. Your bridge page, which reminds the visitor what the video was about and gives the context a 30-second video can't.
  4. The seller's sales page, through your affiliate link.

A bridge page example

Here's a filled-in example for a made-up product, a beginner sourdough baking course. Notice that it doesn't promise results and says what the writer did and didn't check.

HeadlineA beginner-friendly look at learning sourdough at home
IntroductionI make short videos about baking for complete beginners. This page explains the online course I mentioned, so you can decide if it's worth a look.
Who it may suit / who it may notIt may suit you if you've never baked bread and want a step-by-step video course. It may not suit you if you already bake sourdough, or if you'd rather learn from a free book or videos.
What I actually checkedI read the full sales page, the list of lessons and the refund policy. I haven't taken the course myself.Only write what's true. If you have taken it, say what you found, good and bad.
ButtonSee the course page →
Disclosure, directly under the buttonI earn a commission if you buy through this link. It doesn't change the price you pay.Only say it doesn't change the price if that's true for the program.
Three short FAQsDo I need special equipment? Is there a refund policy? How long are the lessons?Answer from the seller's own published information, and link to it where you can.

What every good example has in common

  • A headline about the visitor's problem, not "the best product ever".
  • Who it's not for, said plainly.
  • Only what the writer actually checked.
  • A disclosure right under the button.
  • No income claims, fake timers or invented results.

Do you need a bridge page?

No. You can link straight to an offer, and plenty of affiliates do. A bridge page tends to help when:

  • the product needs more explanation than fits in a short video,
  • you want a clear, visible spot for your disclosure, or
  • you want to be upfront about who the offer is not for.

If the offer is simple and your video already explains it, a bridge page may just be an extra click.

Will a bridge page rank on Google?

Usually not, and it doesn't need to: people reach it from your videos, not from search. Be careful about copying the seller's text onto it, though. Google's spam policies describe thin affiliation as pages with affiliate links where product descriptions and reviews are copied from the merchant without original content or added value. Writing the page in your own words, about what you actually checked, avoids that and is more useful to the visitor anyway.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Copying the seller's logo and design so visitors think they're on the official site.
  • Fake countdown timers, invented scarcity or made-up results.
  • Describing experiences with the product you haven't had.
  • Repeating income or health claims from the sales page as if they were facts.
  • Hiding the disclosure below the fold or in a footer.
  • Ignoring the affiliate program's rules, for example about using the brand's name or trademarks.

Questions beginners ask

How do I make a bridge page for free?

Many link-in-bio tools, one-page site builders and landing page tools have free plans that are enough for a single page. Before you pick one, check that its terms allow affiliate links and that you can put text right under a button for your disclosure.

Do affiliate programs allow bridge pages?

Many do, but rules vary. Some restrict using the brand's name in your page address, copying their images, or making claims they haven't approved. Read the program's terms before you build the page.

Can I use AI to write my bridge page?

You can use it for a first draft, but check every sentence. The page has to be true for you: what you checked, who it suits and your disclosure. Don't let a tool add experiences or results you don't have.

Is a bridge page the same as a funnel?

A funnel is the whole path from content to purchase. A bridge page is one step in it. A simple funnel can be just video, profile link, bridge page and sales page.
